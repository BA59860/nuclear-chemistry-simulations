Part I: Investigation of Alpha Decay via Simulation

Directions: Open the Alpha Decay simulation and use it to answer the following questions.

1. Investigating Alpha Decay:

a.

Click on the Single Atom tab and observe the decay of polonium-211. Use the Reset Nucleus button to watch the process repeatedly. Write a description of what happens during the alpha decay of an atom.

b.

Now check your ideas by selecting the “Custom” atom and letting the simulation run. Reflect on your ideas and write any new ideas you might have below.

c.

Did you find the chart at the top helpful or not? Explain.

d.

Verify your ideas by using the periodic table or other resources to determine what the differences are between polonium (with a mass number of 211) and lead (with a mass number of 207). Also, use other resources to see what alpha decay means and cite at least one valid source.

e.

Practice using your ideas by predicting what would happen if the following nuclei undergo alpha decay. Make sure the nuclear reaction is balanced.

Radium-226 →+
Plutonium-240 →+
Uranium-238 →+

Write each product as name-mass number or symbol-mass number, for example Po-211 or polonium-211.

2. Investigating Half-Life:

Note: Using the Multiple Atoms tab may be helpful.

a.

Use the chart at the top of the simulation to test ideas you might have about half-life. Make sure to use multiple samples and substances with a variety of half-lives. Create a data table below that shows the results of your tests.

b.

In your own words, describe what half-life means.

c.

Without using the simulation, check your ideas by drawing predictions for the following scenario:

Suppose you have a radioactive substance that has a half-life of 1.5 seconds. Make predictions of what will happen by sketching the pie charts indicating the relative number of atoms of the substance and its alpha decay product for a reaction starting with 40 total atoms.

d.

Use the Custom atom in the simulation to test the above scenario. Copy the pie charts into the circles provided below. Note that using the Pause and Step buttons may help.

3.

How do your predictions compare to the results shown in the simulation?

4.

Run the simulation for this scenario repeatedly and compare the results of multiple trials. Use the data table provided to show your results (relative numbers of starting substance and its decay product).

5.

What ideas do you have to explain the similarities and differences in the data and also your predictions?

6.

Try another substance with a different half-life to see if your conclusions make sense. Describe your test, results, and conclusions.

7.

Practice using your ideas: Is it reasonable to assume that if you start with 10 atoms of polonium, that 0.5 s later only 5 will remain? What if you start with 500 atoms? Explain.

Part II: Investigation of Beta Decay via Simulation

Directions: Open the Beta Decay simulation and use it to answer the following questions.

1. Investigating Beta Decay:

a.

Click on the Single Atom tab and observe the decay of hydrogen-3 and carbon-14. Use the Reset Nucleus button to watch the process repeatedly. Write a description of what happens in the beta decay of an atom.

b.

Now check your ideas by selecting the “Custom” atom and letting the simulation run. Reflect on your ideas and write any new ideas you might have below.

c.

Verify your ideas by using online resources to determine what the differences are between hydrogen-3 and helium-3, as well as carbon-14 and nitrogen-14. Also, use other resources to see what beta decay means and cite at least one valid source.

d.

Practice using your ideas by predicting what would happen if the following undergo beta decay:

Scientific note: The original prompt retains carbon-10. It undergoes positron emission (β⁺), whereas the simulation illustrates β⁻ decay. Account for that distinction in your carbon-10 reaction. Reference: MIT radioactive decay notes.

Carbon-10 →+
Cesium-137 →+
Thorium-234 →+
e.

Practice using your ideas by predicting what would happen if uranium-238 undergoes alpha decay, followed by beta decay.

Uranium-238 →+
→+

Use the first line for the alpha decay and the second line for the beta decay that follows.

2. Investigating Half-Life for Beta Decay:

Note: Using the Multiple Atoms tab may be helpful once again.

a.

Use the charts at the top of the simulation to test ideas you might have about half-life. Make sure to use multiple samples and substances with a variety of half-lives. Create a data table below that shows the results of your tests.

b.

In your own words, describe what half-life means for beta decay.

c.

Without using the simulation, check your ideas by drawing predictions for the following scenario:

Suppose you have a radioactive substance that has a half-life of 20 years. Make predictions of what will happen by sketching the pie charts indicating the relative number of atoms of the substance and its beta decay product for a reaction starting with 100 total atoms.

d.

Use the Custom atom in the simulation to test the above scenario. Copy the pie charts into the circles provided below. Note that using the Pause and Step buttons may help.

e.

How do your predictions compare to the results shown in the simulation?

f.

What ideas do you have to explain the similarities and differences in the data and also your predictions?

3.

Compare and contrast the alpha and beta decay processes, including the meaning of half-life for each process.

Part III: Investigation of Radioactive Dating via Simulation

Directions: Open the Radioactive Dating Game simulation and use it to answer the following questions.

1.

Explore the simulation, making sure to try all the tabs. Your goal is to figure out why there is more than one element used to estimate how old things might be.

2.

Which elements’ isotopes are used to estimate how old something is? Why do scientists use more than one type? (Be specific, it is not just to get repeated results!)

3.

Pretend you are a scientist and have a tool like the one on Dating Game tab:

a.

How do you decide which to use: carbon-14 or uranium-238?

b.

How does the percentage help you estimate the age?

c.

If you can’t get a reading on one object like the fish fossil , what else can you try? Determine the approximate age of the fish fossil and explain what you did to estimate the fossil age.

4.

If you were a forensic scientist and found a buried corpse, could you use one of the isotopes in the simulation to figure out how long ago the person died? Explain.

5.

What type of nuclear reaction do carbon-14 and uranium-238 undergo? Explain how you figured this out and write the reaction for each.
